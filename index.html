                    DraftKings
                    latest
                  
                  software engineering intern — market making
                  
                    2026 · jun–aug
                    
                  
                
                
                  Prediction markets — Kalshi, CME, Robinhood — turned sports into something you
                  trade rather than something you bet on. DraftKings had a decade of pricing models built to
                  run a sportsbook, and I worked on the plumbing that turned them into a market maker: quoting both
                  sides of a contract, continuously, fast enough that an exchange will actually take the price.
                
              
              

                

                  

                    

                      A sportsbook and a market maker look similar from the outside and are not the same job. A book
                      posts a line and waits for you. A market maker has to hold a live bid and a live offer on
                      every contract, move both as the game moves, and be there whenever someone wants to trade —
                      that is what "providing liquidity" means, and it is a far harder ask of the systems underneath.
                    

                    

                      Underneath is where I worked. The pricing pipeline carried a decade of sportsbook-shaped
                      assumptions: message topics still fanning out to consumers that no longer existed, an API
                      answering one question by re-reading whole S3 buckets, a mapper that went quiet when a game
                      dropped out instead of saying so. All of that is survivable at sportsbook cadence and a real
                      problem when an exchange is waiting on your quote.
                    

                    

                      I also built an agentic service catalog for the platform, because the on-call engineer at 3am and
                      the new hire in week one turn out to be asking the same questions — and now something
                      answers them.
                    

                  

                  
    
                    
  • +25%pipeline throughput, and ~20k lines of legacy code retired
    • 
                    
  • −70%latency on the endpoint traders and backtests query
    • 
                    
  • 78.7Mwarning logs a week, silenced

                  Duke School of Medicine
                  machine learning research assistant
                  
                    2025/26 · nov–jun
                    
                  
                
                
                  Rats talk in ultrasound, and the way they talk changes when they get sick — before much else
                  does. I trained models to hear that change: neural networks and random forest classifiers that pick
                  out early-stage Krabbe disease from recordings a person would need weeks to label by hand.
                
              
              

                

                  

                    

                      Krabbe disease is degenerative, so the whole study is a race: the earlier you can tell whether a
                      treatment is doing anything, the more of the animal's window you still have to work in. The
                      standard read-outs arrive late. Vocalization changes arrive early — but reading them meant a
                      researcher scrolling through hours of audio drawing boxes around squeaks, one at a time.
                    

                    

                      So the first job was making the data usable at all: a pipeline that turns raw ultrasonic audio
                      into high-resolution sonograms, with adaptive thresholding and Gaussian noise reduction so a faint
                      call doesn't disappear into room noise. Then a Faster R-CNN detector to find the calls and sort
                      them into twenty-odd syllable types, and neural network and random forest classifiers on top of
                      the acoustic features — MFCCs, spectral entropy — to tell a healthy animal from a
                      symptomatic one.
                    

                    

                      The accuracy number was never really the point. The point is that a study which used to be gated
                      on annotation time stopped being gated on annotation time.
                    

                  

                  
    
                    
  • −85%manual annotation time, at 98% recall
    • 
                    
  • 20+vocalization syllable types found automatically
    • 
                    
  • 96%accuracy telling healthy from symptomatic
    •

                  United States Army
                  software engineering intern — resilient networks
                  
                    2025 · may–aug
                    
                  
                
                
                  A swarm of palm-sized drones becomes a very expensive paperweight the moment somebody jams its radio.
                  I worked on the Crazyflie nano-drone platform, building swarm channel-hopping that learns its
                  way around a jammer instead of guessing at it.
                
              
              

                

                  

                    

                      Jamming isn't a wall so much as a moving one. Something parks on a slice of the spectrum and
                      shifts around; the swarm's only real defence is to keep changing which channel it talks on, faster
                      than the jammer can follow — and to have every drone agree on where to hop next, because a
                      swarm that hops out of sync has jammed itself.
                    

                    

                      That's two problems. Timing first: a Bluetooth TSCH clock-sync protocol, so the swarm shares a
                      schedule tight enough to hop together. Then the choosing — which channel next? That is a
                      multi-armed bandit problem:
                      you pick repeatedly between options whose payoff you only learn by trying them, trading off
                      exploiting the channel that has been clean lately against exploring one that might be cleaner.
                      Reinforcement learning does the choosing, with safe-RL risk constraints bounding it so the swarm
                      can never explore its way into a blackout.
                    

                    

                      That last constraint is what became the paper — a JSSR publication I co-authored on bounding
                      communication risk with safe reinforcement learning.
                    

                  

                  
    
                    
  • 95.3%packets delivered under static and dynamic jamming
    • 
                    
  • −30%blackout time, once safe-RL bounded the choices
    • 
                    
  • JSSRpublication co-authored
    •

                  GMU Wireless Cyber Center
                  cyber security lab intern
                  
                    2024 · jun–aug
                    
                  
                
                
                  Consumer drones ship with radios that trust more or less anything that talks to them. Inside a closed
                  lab, I built the tooling that proves it — knocking a drone off its controller and flying it away
                  — so the failure mode is documented rather than theoretical.
                
              
              

                

                  

                    

                      Two halves to it. The first was a network penetration tool in Python driving the Aircrack-ng suite
                      against 802.11: find the link between a drone and its controller, force a deauthentication, and be
                      the thing it reconnects to. Across the target UAVs on the bench that worked about four times in
                      five.
                    

                    

                      The second half was slower and more interesting — taking apart the Parrot Bebop's firmware
                      to find a remote exploit path that didn't depend on the radio trick at all. All of it ran inside
                      the lab, on hardware we owned, which is the only place work like this belongs.
                    

                  

                  
    
                    
  • 80%remote takeover rate across target UAVs in the lab
    • 
                    
  • 802.11deauth path scripted end to end
