                    DraftKings

                  software engineering intern — market making
                  
                    2026 · jun–aug
                    
                  
                
                
                  I was part of the company-wide push to go live market making on prediction exchanges —
                  Kalshi, CME, Robinhood. My piece was the data side: rebuilding how market data moves through the
                  pricing stack so the automated trading systems get faster, richer signals when a market starts
                  turning. We did about $30M in volume while I was there.
                
              
              

                

                  

                    

                      Running a sportsbook and running a market maker aren't the same job, even though they look
                      similar from the outside. A sportsbook posts a line and waits for you to take it. A market maker
                      has to have real money on both sides of every contract, all the time, and keep moving those
                      prices as the game plays out — automatically, with nobody in the loop. Same models
                      underneath, much more demanding systems.
                    

                    

                      Prices and game events moved through a pipeline built for a sportsbook, so it was slower than we
                      needed and didn't carry enough with it — by the time a signal that a market was getting
                      dangerous reached the pricing engine, it was late and pretty thin. I reworked how that data flows so
                      those signals land faster and with more context attached, and so everything downstream is
                      looking at the same picture. A lot of dead weight came out along the
                      way: topics still publishing to services that didn't exist anymore, an API answering one question
                      by scanning whole S3 buckets, a mapper that just went quiet when a game dropped off instead of
                      telling anyone.
                    

                    

                      I also built an internal AI assistant for the platform that answers "what does this service
                      actually do" questions, since the person on call at 3am and the new hire in week one turn out to
                      be asking the same things.
                    

                  

                  
    
                    
  • $30Min contracts traded once we were live
    • 
                    
  • −70%latency on the endpoint the trading systems hit most
    • 
                    
  • +25%pipeline throughput, plus ~20k lines of old code deleted

                  Duke School of Medicine
                  machine learning research assistant
                  
                    2025/26 · nov–jun
                    
                  
                
                
                  Rats squeak at frequencies we can't hear, and the way they squeak changes when they get sick, well
                  before most other symptoms show up. I trained models to catch that change, so researchers can spot
                  early Krabbe disease in a recording instead of labeling audio by hand for weeks.
                
              
              

                

                  

                    

                      Krabbe is degenerative, so timing matters a lot. The sooner you can tell whether a treatment is
                      doing anything, the more of the animal's life you still have to work with. The usual measurements
                      show up late; the vocal changes show up early. The catch was that reading them meant someone
                      sitting there scrolling through hours of audio, drawing a box around each squeak one at a time.
                    

                    

                      So the first job was making the audio usable. I built a pipeline that turns the raw recordings
                      into clean, high-resolution sonograms and filters out room noise without losing the quieter
                      calls. Then a detector that finds each call and sorts it into one of about twenty types, and a
                      couple of classifiers on top of that to tell a healthy rat from a symptomatic one.
                    

                    

                      The accuracy number is nice, but the real win is that the study isn't stuck waiting on someone to
                      label audio anymore.
                    

                  

                  
    
                    
  • −85%time spent labeling audio by hand, at 98% recall
    • 
                    
  • 20+types of squeak sorted automatically
    • 
                    
  • 96%accuracy telling a healthy rat from a sick one
    •

                  United States Army
                  software engineering intern — resilient networks
                  
                    2025 · may–aug
                    
                  
                
                
                  Jam the radio on a group of small drones and the whole thing is useless. I worked on getting them to
                  hop between radio channels to stay ahead of a jammer — and, more importantly, to
                  learn which channel to jump to next instead of guessing.
                
              
              

                

                  

                    

                      A jammer isn't a fixed obstacle — it moves around the spectrum looking for you. So the
                      drones have to keep switching channels faster than it can follow, and all of them have to agree
                      on where they're going next. A group that hops out of sync has basically jammed itself.
                    

                    

                      That's two separate problems. The timing one I handled with a Bluetooth clock-sync protocol, so
                      every drone is working off the same schedule. The other one is picking the next channel, which is
                      a classic multi-armed bandit
                      setup: you're choosing between options over and over, and you only find out how good an option
                      is by trying it. Do you stick with the channel that's been clean, or test one that might be
                      better?
                      Reinforcement learning makes that call, with safety limits on top so the drones can't experiment
                      their way into losing contact completely.
                    

                    

                      That last part is what turned into a paper — I co-authored a JSSR publication on it.
                    

                  

                  
    
                    
  • 95.3%of packets still got through while being jammed
    • 
                    
  • −30%time spent out of contact, once the safety limits were in
    • 
                    
  • JSSRpaper co-authored
    •

                  cyber security lab intern
                  
                    2024 · jun–aug
                    
                  
                
                
                  Consumer drones will more or less talk to anyone who asks. In a closed lab I built the tooling that
                  shows it — knocking a drone off its controller and flying it away myself — so it's a
                  documented problem instead of a hypothetical one.
                
              
              

                

                  

                    

                      Two parts to it. The first was a Python tool driving Aircrack-ng at the Wi-Fi link between a
                      drone and its controller: find the connection, force it to drop, then be the thing it reconnects
                      to. On the drones we had on the bench, that worked about four times out of five.
                    

                    

                      The second part was slower and honestly more fun — pulling apart the Parrot Bebop's
                      firmware looking for a way in that didn't depend on the Wi-Fi trick at all. All of it ran inside
                      the lab on hardware we owned, which is the only place this kind of work belongs.
